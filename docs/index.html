A generative model of future dynamics

Learned dynamics models can forecast the evolution of a physical system or predict the consequences of actions before a robot executes them. In both settings, the prediction must remain meaningful over an entire trajectory. Small errors can accumulate as a model rolls forward, and partial observations or stochastic dynamics can leave several continuations consistent with the available information. The modeling problem is to capture this temporal evolution together with its uncertainty.

Forecasting methods differ in both what they predict and how they generate it. A deterministic predictor returns one continuation; a probabilistic predictor represents a conditional distribution over continuations. Either can operate autoregressively. Among generative approaches, autoregressive diffusion samples the next state from the evolving history, full-sequence diffusion jointly denoises a chosen future horizon, and block methods generate successive chunks of the future. These approaches organize temporal dependence and computation differently, even when they target the same forecasting distribution.

Probabilistic prediction matters when the observations do not determine a unique outcome. An unobserved dynamical parameter, for example, can produce distinct future motions even when the recent visible histories are closely aligned. Averaging those motions can yield a path unlike any likely realization. A useful generative model should therefore capture both the range of outcomes and their dependence across time: each sample should describe a complete continuation of the system.

Diffusion and flow-matching forecasters typically generate samples along an auxiliary time coordinate, with repeated network evaluations to transform noise into a prediction. Streaming Flow (SF) makes a different choice: its integration time is the physical forecast time. It learns a velocity field that follows observed motion while contracting perturbations around training trajectories. Integrating this field directly advances the forecast, so forward Euler requires one velocity-network evaluation per step. The denoising construction and temporal evolution share one clock.

The standard non-latent formulation still produces one trajectory from a fixed state and history. Randomizing the starting state can create different paths, but then the physical state also has to encode unresolved future variation. Near a branch, separating those futures through small state differences can make the field sensitive to perturbations. This motivates a separate variable that conditions the dynamics while leaving the observed starting state unchanged.

We introduce Variational Streaming Flow (VSF), a variational latent-variable formulation of physical-time forecasting. During training, an encoder infers latent codes from realized local motion, and the velocity model learns how its predictions depend on those codes. At inference, a single draw from the prior conditions the entire rollout. Repeated state updates then define a distribution over complete future trajectories, with one field evaluation per Euler step for each sample. We study this formulation on long-horizon dynamical-system forecasts and as an action-conditioned predictor in a visual world model.

Read the paper abstract

Probabilistic forecasting is important for predicting complex dynamical systems because intrinsic randomness and incomplete observations can cause the same observed state to evolve into multiple plausible futures. While flow matching is a flexible approach for probabilistic forecasting, it is computationally expensive. Streaming flow (SF) reformulates this approach to model temporal evolution efficiently by learning a continuous velocity field directly in physical time. However, SF learns a deterministic velocity field. Thus, it provides only a single future trajectory for a given fixed initial state and observation history. To overcome this limitation, we introduce Variational Streaming Flow (VSF). Our approach learns a latent distribution that is conditioned on the dynamics of interest. In turn, this enables probabilistic forecasting. Importantly, we retain the computational efficiency of SF by generating in physical time. Across deterministic and stochastic dynamical systems, VSF demonstrates superior predictive accuracy and distributional fidelity. We demonstrate the advantage for both long-horizon rollouts exceeding 1,000 steps, and settings with bifurcating dynamics. Moreover, VSF can be integrated into existing Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)-based world models as a plug-and-play predictor to improve temporal dynamics and goal-directed success rate in navigation, motion planning, and manipulation.

What this makes possible

A forecasting model has to preserve meaningful dynamics as its own predictions become the context for later steps. For deterministic systems, this means tracking the reference trajectory as small errors accumulate. When a parameter is hidden, it means generating the range of continuations supported by the observations. The computational cost becomes consequential when a model generates many such paths, either to estimate a distribution or to evaluate candidate actions. VSF uses one velocity-network evaluation per Euler step for each sample, while a persistent latent code conditions the full continuation. We evaluate this combination on 1,000-step physical-system forecasts and on four tasks in which a visual world model predicts action outcomes. 1 · Generating a forecast

Two clocks in generative forecasting

A diffusion forecast has two time coordinates with different roles. Physical time orders the states of the system; generative time indexes the refinement of noise into those states. An autoregressive diffusion model completes this refinement for the next state before moving on. A block model refines several future states together, then conditions the next block on what it has generated. Full-sequence diffusion refines a requested horizon jointly, so temporal interactions can be modeled across that horizon during generation. Streaming Flow instead makes each integration step a step along the forecast itself. The animations below show when each method commits to a future state and which computation must finish before that state becomes available.

All four approaches generate complete trajectories. Joint generation avoids recursively generating individual physical-time states, but requires a requested horizon to refine together. Block and autoregressive generation can extend the forecast while repeatedly using their own outputs as context. Their refinement cost recurs for each block or state. Streaming Flow removes this inner loop, with one field evaluation per Euler step. Evaluation counts alone do not determine wall-clock speed: a joint pass processes a longer sequence, and architecture, batching, and sample count also matter. 2 · Physical-time flow

What is Streaming Flow?

Streaming Flow learns the rate of change of a state and integrates that rate forward from the latest observation. Here, a velocity is the derivative of the modeled state; the state may contain physical variables or learned visual features. Training only on points along an observed trajectory would leave the field's behavior at nearby states unspecified, even though a forecast will visit such states as errors accumulate. SF therefore constructs a contracting tube around each training path. Its velocity target combines the motion along the path with a correction that pulls nearby states toward it. The learned field is trained on these local targets, and numerical integration turns the field into a complete forecast.

Build training examples around a path

Start with an observed trajectory, ξ(s), and add Gaussian perturbations around it. Their scale decreases as physical time advances, defining a tube of nearby training states. The model must learn motion within this neighborhood as well as on the reference path. The tube is a training construction around one realization; its shrinking width does not imply that uncertainty between future realizations should disappear.

Ys = ξ(s) + σ(s)ε, σ(s) = σ0e−κs

Learn one physical-time field

The target field combines the reference velocity with a restoring correction, gξ(x, s) = ξ̇(s) − κ(x − ξ(s)). On the path, the correction vanishes. Away from it, the target contracts the displacement while continuing to move forward in physical time. Standard SF fits a neural field to these targets by squared-error regression.

LSF = E‖vθ − gξ‖²

Roll forward one step at a time

At inference, start from the latest observation and evaluate the learned field at the current predicted state. The field also receives normalized physical time sk and a history representation h. An Euler update advances the forecast directly, and generated states supply the rolling history for later updates. For a fixed starting state and history, this non-latent procedure produces one continuation.

x̂k+1 = x̂k + Δt vθ(x̂k, sk, h)

3 · Bifurcation example

How a latent code separates futures

The paper uses a two-branch construction to isolate a difficulty in representing uncertainty through the physical state alone. Two trajectories share the same past and separate after a specified time; the observed history contains no information about the branch. A non-latent field has only the current state, time, and history on which to base its prediction. It can separate branches by responding to small state perturbations, but those perturbations must remain distinguishable as the training tube contracts. In this example, improving fidelity to the shared path therefore increases sensitivity near the separator. A latent-conditioned field can assign the branch choice to a separate coordinate, allowing the physical starting state to remain the same for both continuations.

4 · Method

Variational Streaming Flow

VSF trains a variational encoder to infer latent codes from realized local motion, then learns a velocity field conditioned on those codes. The velocity network receives the sampled code together with the current state, time, and history. KL regularization encourages the inferred codes to remain close to the prior available at forecast time, when the future motion is unknown. Inference uses that prior directly: we draw one code and retain it as the predicted state and history evolve. The contribution is this variational learning formulation for physical-time forecasting; auxiliary-latent extensions of Streaming Flow also exist, as discussed in the paper.

The forecast follows the learned latent-conditioned dynamics:

d x̂s / ds = vθ(x̂s, s, h, z)

Training

Sample a transition from an observed training trajectory to obtain a local state and physical velocity (xs, us). An encoder infers codes that explain this motion.

z(1:M) iid∼ qφ(· | us, xs, s, h)

Evaluate the corresponding physical-time velocities.

ûs(1:M) = vθ(xs, s, h, z(1:M))

Optimize generalized energy reconstruction together with KL regularization toward p(z).

Inference

Sample z ∼ p(z) and retain it throughout the forecast.

Advance the predicted state directly in physical time.

x̂k+1 = x̂k + Δs vθ(x̂k, sk, h, z)

Append generated states to the rolling history and refresh h at each segment boundary.

Local supervision, complete forecasts

Training and generation operate at different scales. A training example supplies local motion from an observed trajectory, which supervises the velocity network at a state, time, and history. During generation, that network is repeatedly evaluated at its own predicted states. Each prediction affects the next state and, through the rolling history, later predictions. The same latent code conditions every update. For a fixed history and code, this procedure produces one complete path; drawing new codes induces a distribution over paths:

z ∼ p(z), x̂m+1:T = Rolloutθ(xa:m, z)

The code is retained across segment boundaries, including updates to the history representation. This makes the rollout a model of complete future trajectories, even though its training targets are local velocities. The quality of that path distribution still has to be established: accurate local distributions alone do not prove that the generated joint distribution across time is correct. We evaluate complete rollouts in the stochastic experiments, while the theoretical result below concerns velocity distributions at a fixed local context.

Latent velocity model

At one physical time, let cs = (xs, s, h) collect the state, time, and history. The model assigns a distribution to the velocity us by integrating over latent codes. The training encoder approximates which codes could explain a realized velocity, making this latent-variable model tractable to train. It is used only when the target motion is available; inference samples from the prior and follows the learned field.

maximize log pθ(us | cs), where pθ(us | cs) = ∫ pθ(us | cs, z)p(z) dz

The paper first derives an evidence lower bound (ELBO) for this conditional velocity likelihood. A Gaussian reconstruction model leads to a squared-error term plus a KL penalty. This derivation explains the roles of the encoder and prior. The final objective below replaces the Gaussian reconstruction with a distributional energy term and retains the KL regularizer.

Learning a distribution of local motion

At each sampled transition, the encoder proposes several codes and the velocity network produces a corresponding ensemble. Generalized energy reconstruction evaluates these M velocity samples together. Its two terms balance agreement with the observed target against the distances between predictions. Repeated over training examples, this provides a distributional learning signal. The choice of reconstruction term matters when a code leaves some variation in motion unresolved.

Why not use MSE alone?

For a fixed context and code, the optimal squared-error predictor is v*(c, z) = E[U | C = c, Z = z]. A sufficiently informative code can resolve that ambiguity, but unresolved variation is averaged. This is why adding a latent input and choosing a distributional training objective are separate design choices.

LES(s) = (1/M) Σi ‖ûs(i) − us‖₂α − (1/2M²) Σi,j ‖ûs(i) − ûs(j)‖₂α, 0 < α < 2

In the paper's formulation above, the first term measures distance to the observed velocity, and the subtracted pairwise term accounts for diversity among samples. Increasing spread alone does not minimize the objective: samples that move too far from the target also increase the first term. The exponent controls how distances are weighted. The full loss retains KL regularization to encourage compatibility between the training posteriors and the prior used for forecasting:

LVSF = E[LES(s) + βKL KL(qφ(z | us, xs, s, h) ‖ p(z))]

What does aggregate posterior matching mean?

We construct a two-branch example to make q̄φ(z | c) = p(z) concrete:

  • Inference prior: p(z) = (0.50, 0.50), so either branch is sampled with equal probability.
  • Training posteriors: the encoder assigns (0.85, 0.15) for U = −1 and (0.15, 0.85) for U = +1, ordered as (P(Z = −1), P(Z = +1)).
  • Aggregate posterior: averaging over the two equally likely targets recovers the inference prior.
q̄φ(z | c) = ½(0.85, 0.15) + ½(0.15, 0.85) = (0.50, 0.50) = p(z).

5 · Results

Dynamical-system forecasting

A long forecast exposes errors that are difficult to see in a single transition. For autoregressive and physical-time models, each predicted state influences the next prediction, so an early error can alter the rest of the rollout. We evaluate this accumulated effect over 1,000 steps on the double pendulum, Rössler, and Lorenz systems. The experiments separate two questions: how accurately a model follows a unique continuation when the full state is observed, and how well it represents uncertainty when a dynamical parameter is hidden. A probabilistic model should concentrate around the reference trajectory in the first setting and recover the distribution of continuations in the second.

The comparison includes diffusion and rectified-flow forecasters, the recurrent probabilistic model DeepAR, standard SF, and the pretrained time-series model Chronos-2. SF and VSF share the same U-Net backbone. The SF (perturb.) baseline adds Gaussian noise to the initial state at inference, testing whether perturbing the physical state is sufficient to produce useful forecast variation. Chronos-2 remains frozen; the calibrated variant applies a post-hoc correction to its predictive quantiles. The tables summarize performance across the test data, while the animations expose the behavior of selected rollouts.

5.1

Tracking a single future

Fully observed deterministic dynamics define one future for each initial condition. Chaotic behavior refers to sensitivity to small perturbations; it does not introduce randomness into the simulator. A probabilistic forecaster should therefore produce samples concentrated around the unique reference trajectory. Maintaining that concentration over 1,000 steps requires accurate local motion and a rollout that remains close to the true dynamics. We use the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) to evaluate the forecast ensemble, and RMSE and MAE to evaluate its mean. Looking at both matters because an accurate ensemble average can conceal individual samples that follow the wrong motion.

The comparison with SF is informative because both models advance directly in physical time. VSF's lower errors show that its gains extend to settings with a unique target trajectory. Adding initial-state noise to SF reduces some errors, but does not close the gap in these experiments. The Chronos-2 results describe performance with the short forecasting context used here; this is a comparison within that setup.

5.2

Forecasting with unresolved dynamics

Here uncertainty has a known source: a dynamical parameter is withheld from the forecaster. For each initial state, we draw that parameter independently for each reference trajectory and hold it fixed during the simulation. The resulting motion is deterministic once the parameter is specified, but different values can produce closely aligned observed histories followed by different futures. This construction lets us compare forecast samples with an ensemble generated by the simulator. It also gives the persistent latent code a concrete role: a sampled continuation should retain a consistent dynamical character as it evolves.

A useful forecast must recover the range of plausible motion while respecting the system's dynamics. Samples that all follow one continuation miss uncertainty; samples that spread by drifting away from the dynamics also give a poor distribution. Conditional mean error (CME) and conditional standard-deviation error (CSE) measure whether the ensemble has the right center and spread at each forecast time. Standard SF has CSE equal to one here because its repeated forecasts from a fixed input have zero spread while the reference ensemble varies.

Matching these time-local summaries does not determine how states are connected across a trajectory. For that reason, the table also reports Wasserstein distance (W1) and energy distance (ED), treating each complete rollout as one ordered vector. These comparisons retain cross-time dependence and assess the distribution of complete future paths. They connect the local velocity model to the trajectories it produces when run forward.

6 · World-model planning

Planning with predicted futures

In model-based control, a forecast is part of the decision procedure. The planner predicts the consequences of candidate actions, assigns a cost to each predicted outcome, and executes the plan it prefers. Errors in that prediction can change which action appears best, even when average forecasting error is small. This makes planning a different test from reproducing a held-out trajectory: the predictor is evaluated on action sequences selected using its own forecasts. It also makes inference cost consequential, because the search repeats these predictions many times before acting. We test whether VSF improves goal-directed behavior under the same visual representation and planning budget as the existing world model.

We use LeWM, a world model built on a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA). Its encoder maps images of the current state and goal into a learned feature space. The predictor receives the current state features and a candidate action sequence, then forecasts how the features evolve under those actions. The planning cost measures the distance between the predicted endpoint and the encoded goal. The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) repeatedly samples action sequences, retains the best candidates, and updates its sampling distribution before choosing a plan.

We freeze the pretrained representation and compare LeWM's autoregressive predictor with SF and VSF predictors trained on offline observation–action trajectories. The encoder, CEM planner, evaluation tasks, and planning budget remain fixed. In VSF, the evolving state features and the sampled uncertainty code have separate roles: the state records the predicted visual configuration, while the code conditions one complete rollout. Each imagined transition uses one velocity-field evaluation. Success rate measures whether the resulting actions reach the goal; prediction RMSE measures agreement with the observed feature trajectory along the selected actions.

Values are mean ± population standard deviation over three seeds, with 50 goals per seed. Success is the percentage of goals reached within a 50-step execution budget. RMSE measures error in the predicted visual state representations along the action sequence selected by CEM. Higher success and lower RMSE are better.

These results show that replacing the dynamics predictor can improve decisions without changing the encoder or search procedure. Prediction error and success remain different measurements: on Cube, SF has slightly lower RMSE, while VSF reaches more goals. RMSE is measured along the action sequence each planner selects, so the methods need not be evaluated on identical trajectories. 7 · Next steps

What comes next

The current experiments use adjacent observations to learn local motion and a persistent code to condition a complete rollout. Extending this formulation to abrupt events, disturbances that arrive during evolution, and decisions that depend on rare outcomes raises different questions about model structure and computational cost.

Physical-time learning works most directly when adjacent states resolve a meaningful local direction. An impact, a contact transition, or strong observation noise can make that direction difficult to estimate. Hybrid dynamics that combine continuous evolution with explicit jumps could address abrupt events. Higher-order or adaptive solvers offer a separate way to reduce integration error when the learned field changes rapidly. Such solvers require additional field evaluations, so their value should be measured through the accuracy they achieve for a given computational budget.

The hidden-parameter experiments study uncertainty that persists throughout a trajectory. Other systems also receive new disturbances during evolution. Combining a persistent code with stochastic innovations would make both sources of variation explicit. Updating the forecast distribution as new observations arrive is another extension, especially when an agent can reduce its uncertainty by interacting with the system.

For planning, the next question is how to use the forecast distribution when selecting an action. Evaluating several continuations for each candidate would allow a planner to assess the variability of its outcome as well as its expected cost. A useful experiment would compare such a planner with the current goal-matching procedure under equal computational budgets, including tasks where a rare failure is much more costly than a small average error.
